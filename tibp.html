Labour promised us modern railways in 2024 – so why does it feel like the 1940s again?

Privatisation and public ownership have both been sold as ways to modernise Britain’s railways. What would count as progress for passengers?

Christian Jeys5 min read

“A new, modern and joined-up railway system, owned by the public and run for the public.” That was Labour MP Peter Swallow’s comment, from the 9th of December last year, describing Labour’s plans to bring passenger rail services contacted by the Department for Transport into public ownership as contracts end. Nationalisation of rail has been a Labour commitment since 2015, adopted under former leader, Jeremy Corbyn. It sounds great! We’ve all experienced years of trains that are late, crowded, or sometimes, never turn up. So, a new system that reverses the privatisation strategy of John Major is the magic key, right? Right?

In December 1946, Alfred Barnes made the case for a “properly integrated system” of busses and trains connecting more easily. Sounds familiar! Following that, the Transport Act 1947 brought the Big Four railway companies into public ownership.

Four decades later, Margaret Thatcher’s Conservative Party presented state ownership of businesses as a drag on efficiency and in 1987, she credited privatisation with improving efficiency and allowing anyone to have a share in those new businesses. So much so, that the Conservatives described privatisation and the opportunity for anyone to purchase shares in the new companies, as the “real public ownership”. At the time, Secretary of State for Transport John MacGregor argued that privatisation of the rail industry would create “greater competition and choice” and would be “the most radical changes to Britain’s railways since 1948” and a modernising step. That was 1992.

Skip to 2024, Louise Haigh calls Labour’s nationalisation plan, “A modern rail system to serve a modern Britain”. In the 1990s, ministers argued that the railway needed more competition and commercial incentives and the state monopoly had too little incentive to respond to passengers. Now, Labour argues that it needs a single organisation with the power to make decisions with less fragmentation, the same argument from 1946. Both the Labour Party of the 2020s and the Conservative Party of the 1990s promised a railway that would be more “modern”, “publicly owned” and would work better for passengers. So, who is right when these arguments become circular? Indeed, what does ‘modern’ actually mean?

In 1992, the Conservatives asked passengers to see competition as the way out of an outdated system. Now, Labour asks the public to see that same system as the source of the problem. The word “modern” is seemingly deployed by politicians from all sides, even when they contradict each other. It has become an empty signifier, its promise of a newer and better policy has broad appeal, but rival parties can present different ways of achieving that goal, even if the policy doesn’t fix the underlying issue.

This has happened before. In 1997, Labour presented “The New NHS” and promised to replace the NHS internal market with cooperation. By 2011, David Cameron said his government would modernise the NHS by widening patient choice and opening up competition. Both promised modernisation and better care but gave differing views on how to get there. This technique brings people together by exploiting a common frustration. Labour exploited frustration with long waits for hospital treatments in the 1990s, and did it again in 2024 with train delays and cancellations.

Even the term, “public ownership”, can mean different things. Thatcher’s Conservatives used it to describe giving people the chance to buy shares. Labour uses it to describe the state owning rail operators on the public’s behalf. When a politician describes a policy as “modernising”, it invites the public to view that idea as the logical progression. It seems that all current politicians in Britain claim to be modernisers, even when they are citing an argument that has been used time and time again. So, who do we believe and more importantly, will the public benefit?

Great British Railways was first proposed by the Conservatives in 2021, promising a “modern” and “joined up” railway with a simpler ticket process and better coordination, while keeping private companies as contracted operators. Therefore, public ownership and a joined-up railway are wholly separate choices, you can have one without the other. The parties agreed that “modernisation” meant more unified and centralised control, but Labour also believes that “modern” needs to go as far as nationalisation. Therefore, modernisation, to Labour, is more than just deeper cooperation and coordination as the Conservatives described, but a question relating to ownership and, for Labour, this is the State.

But nationalisation isn’t having the positive impact Labour politicians would want. In fact, all rail companies have had their Public Performance Measure (PPM) drop since 2024, but the nationalised companies have seen the largest deterioration in performance. The Office of Rail and Road tracks the performance of train operators in the UK and publishes this data. Between the four weeks ending 17 August 2024 and those ending 17 October 2026, PPM fell by an average of 4.68 percentage points across the six operators that had moved into public ownership, compared with 3.26 points across the four still privately run. So, with the exception of trains being painted a different colour, nationalisation has provided non benefit for the commuter.

So, Labour has revived the 1940s idea that the railway works better when it can be planned as a whole and owned by the State. Whether that counts as progress in the modern day waits to be seen and comes down to something voters will judge for themselves: can they afford the ticket, and can they count on their train turning up? Will this be an effective policy that betters the lives of voters, or just choice grounded in ideology over logic. Those who remember British Rail from the 1970s fear that this new venture could suffer the same chronic underfunding that resulted in another modernization in the 1980s. Are we progressing or regressing? Only time will tell.
